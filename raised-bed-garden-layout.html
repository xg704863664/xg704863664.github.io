Raised Bed Garden Layout: Spacing and Planning

Layout decides how much work a garden is. Get four numbers right — bed width, path width, plant spacing and orientation — and everything else becomes easier.

The four numbers

NumberValueWhy
Bed width1.2 m / 4 ft maxReach the centre from both sides without stepping in
Path width45 cm / 18 inWheelbarrow and kneeling room; 90 cm for wheelchair access
Plant spacingVaries — see table belowOvercrowding cuts yield more than it adds
Bed orientationLong axis east–westLess mutual shading through the day

Spacing that actually matters

Seed packets give row spacing meant for farm rows. In a raised bed you use intensive spacing — closer within the bed, but never so close that air cannot move.

CropSpacingPer 1.2 × 2.4 m bed
Tomatoes (indeterminate)45–60 cm4–6 plants, staked
Peppers30–40 cm8–10 plants
Lettuce20–25 cm24–30 plants
Carrots5–8 cmDense rows, thin to 5 cm
Bush beans10–15 cm40–60 plants
Courgette / zucchini90 cm2 plants — they get huge
Onions10 cm60–80 sets
Garlic15 cm40–50 cloves

Three layouts that work

1. Single row down the middle

Best for one tall crop: tomatoes, climbing beans on a trellis, or peppers. Simple to water and harvest, but wastes the edges.

2. Grid (square foot)

Divide the bed into 30 cm squares. Each square holds one large plant (pepper), four medium (lettuce), nine small (carrots) or sixteen tiny (radish).

Good for beginners because spacing decisions are already made, and it makes succession planting obvious — when a square finishes, replant it.

3. Beds split by height

Put tall crops on the north side (in the northern hemisphere) and short crops south of them. Nothing gets shaded, and you can walk the whole bed.

What to plant together

Some pairings genuinely help; most traditional companion planting claims do not survive testing. The ones worth using:

  • Basil with tomatoes — both like the same conditions, and basil is said to repel whitefly
  • Marigolds throughout — French marigolds suppress root-knot nematodes
  • Nasturtiums as a trap crop — aphids prefer them to your vegetables
  • Legumes before leafy crops — beans and peas fix nitrogen that lettuce then uses

See the companion planting chart for the fuller list, including the pairings that are myths.

Rotation in a small garden

With only two or three beds you cannot rotate properly, but you can still avoid the worst problems:

  • Never follow tomatoes with tomatoes — same family (Solanaceae), same soil diseases
  • Follow heavy feeders with light feeders — after tomatoes, plant carrots or beans
  • Follow legumes with leafy greens — the nitrogen is already there

Common questions

How wide should a raised bed be?

1.2 m (4 ft) maximum so you can reach the centre from both sides. If the bed is against a wall or fence, make it 60 cm.

How far apart should raised bed rows be?

In a raised bed you do not need farm-style rows. Space plants by their mature width plus a few centimetres for airflow - typically 20-25 cm for lettuce, 30-40 cm for peppers, 45-60 cm for tomatoes.

Which direction should raised beds run?

Long axis east to west in the northern hemisphere, so plants do not shade each other as the sun moves. On a slope, run them across the slope to reduce erosion.

How many plants fit in a 1.2 x 2.4 m raised bed?

Roughly 24-30 lettuce, 8-10 peppers, 4-6 staked tomatoes, or 40-60 bush beans, depending on the crop and how intensively you space them.
