A Self-Sufficient Backyard: Where to Start

Self-sufficiency on a normal-sized lot is less about growing everything and more about wasting nothing. Three things do most of the work.

1. One real food bed

Not six beds you cannot keep up with. One bed, deep enough to hold moisture, close enough to the house that you actually water it.

Size it at roughly 1m x 2m for a first year. The details that matter - soil mix, depth, what to grow - are in raised bed soil mix and starting a vegetable garden.

2. A compost pile that actually heats up

Roughly equal parts greens (kitchen scraps, grass) and browns (cardboard, leaves, straw), turned occasionally.

  • About one cubic metre is the smallest size that heats properly - smaller piles just sit there
  • If it smells sour, it is too wet; add browns
  • If nothing is happening, it is too dry or lacks nitrogen; add greens and water
  • A pile that heats to 55-65C kills most weed seeds and pathogens

3. Somewhere to store food

The harvest arrives faster than you can eat it, and this is where most first-year gardens lose their value. Plan shelves, jars or a cool corner before the glut, not after.

Shelf life varies far more than people assume - brown rice goes rancid in six months while white rice keeps for years. The food storage chart has the numbers for pantry, fridge and freezer.

Then, in order

Rainwater

A water butt on a downpipe is the cheapest yield improvement you can make. Rainwater is also better for plants than tap water in most areas. One 200-litre butt fills surprisingly fast off a small roof.

Herbs

The best effort-to-value crop in a small space: expensive to buy, easy to grow, useful daily. Nine medicinal plants worth growing covers the ones that earn their space.

Chickens

Only after the first three are running. They need daily attention in winter, without exception. If you are still deciding, backyard chickens for beginners sets out what the commitment actually involves.

Fruit

Last, because it is slow. A fruit tree is several years before a real harvest - worth planting, but not what makes a backyard productive this year.

What it costs

Timber for one bed, a compost bin and a water butt is the main outlay. After that it is seed and compost each year. The equipment people buy and never use - tillers, greenhouses, raised bed kits - is where the money actually goes.

The honest part

A backyard like this does not make you self-sufficient. It replaces part of your vegetable spending, gives you better food, and means a supply problem at the supermarket is an inconvenience rather than a crisis. That is a reasonable goal, and it is achievable on a normal lot.

Common questions

What should a beginner do first for a self-sufficient backyard?

One food bed, a compost pile and somewhere to store the harvest. Everything else is easier once those are running.

How big should a compost pile be?

About one cubic metre - the smallest size that heats up properly.

Do you need a lot of land to be self-sufficient?

No. A normal suburban lot can produce a meaningful share of vegetables and herbs. How the space is used matters more than its size.

How much does a backyard setup cost to start?

Timber for one bed, a compost bin and a water butt. Ongoing costs are seed and compost, not equipment.
